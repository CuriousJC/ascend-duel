# Build Aspects

These nine aspects describe the fundamental priorities a player can build toward.
Individual mechanics are the tools that support those priorities.

The aspects overlap intentionally. They are a way to examine build variety,
tradeoffs, and design coverage—not nine independent stats.

| Aspect              | Fundamental question                                          | Mechanics underneath it                                               |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Economy**         | What can I afford over the run?                               | Vitae generation, interest, discounts, shop support.                  |
| **Composition**     | What kinds of cards does my build want?                       | Forms, elements, specific concepts, specialization versus diversity.  |
| **Hands**           | What combinations am I trying to assemble and reward?         | Hand patterns, multipliers, stones, hand-specific relics.             |
| **Action capacity** | How much can I commit in one turn?                            | AP, cost reductions, effortless cards, attack/defend allocation.      |
| **Tempo**           | How quickly must my build become effective?                   | Round limit, immediate power versus buildup, preparation turns.       |
| **Consistency**     | How reliably can I access what my build needs?                | Deck size, removal, duplication, discards, hand size.                 |
| **Offense**         | How much damage does my committed turn produce?               | Base DMG, card strength, repeated hits, offensive bonuses and growth. |
| **Defense**         | How do I survive long enough to execute the build?            | Shields, health, healing, defensive effects.                          |
| **Engine capacity** | How many supporting effects and resources can my build carry? | Relic slots, weightless relics, consumable capacity.                  |

## What Each Aspect Covers

### Economy

Economy governs acquiring power across the run: generating vitae, preserving it
for interest, and spending it efficiently.

Shop support belongs here, but economy is broader than the shop. A card that
generates vitae and a tonic that reduces prices both improve purchasing power
through different mechanisms.

### Composition

Composition is the identity of the deck: what it contains and what properties
the build rewards.

A player might specialize in crush, lightning, a particular card concept, or a
specific combination such as lightning crush cards. Diversity can also be a
composition goal when effects reward multiple forms or elements.

### Hands

Hands concern the combinations the player wants to assemble and the rewards
attached to those combinations.

Composition asks which cards belong in the deck. Hands ask which relationships
between those cards the player wants to produce. Improving a hand's multiplier
and making that hand easier to assemble are related investments, but they
operate through different aspects.

### Action Capacity

Action capacity determines how much of the available hand can be committed
during a turn.

AP, cheaper cards, and cards exempt from the action count can all expand that
capacity. The player also decides how much capacity to allocate to attacks
versus defenses.

This is distinct from hand size: having more options available does not
necessarily mean being able to play more of them.

### Tempo

Tempo concerns when power becomes available relative to the fight's deadline.

An immediately strong build and a build that becomes stronger after repeated
hits may produce similar eventual damage but perform differently within a
limited number of rounds. Preparation and defensive turns consume opportunities
to finish the fight.

Tempo refers to progress through game turns and rounds, not animation speed or
how quickly the player operates the interface.

### Consistency

Consistency determines how reliably the player can access and assemble the
cards their build needs.

Deck size is one lever, alongside removal, duplication, discards, and hand size.
A larger deck is not automatically worse: what matters is the probability of
finding useful cards and combinations.

Composition describes what the deck wants to contain; consistency describes
how reliably it delivers.

### Offense

Offense is the damage produced by the cards and effects the player commits.

Its mechanisms include base DMG, individual card strength, flat bonuses,
multipliers, repeated hits, and growth. Those mechanisms can interact
differently even when they all increase the final damage total.

Hands remain a separate aspect because choosing and developing a combination
is a substantial build commitment of its own.

### Defense

Defense preserves the opportunity to execute the build.

Shields, maximum health, healing, and defensive effects provide different ways
to survive. Defense can also support other priorities: preserving health can
improve rewards, while surviving another turn can let a growing engine mature.

### Engine Capacity

Engine capacity governs how many supporting effects and resources can coexist
in a build.

It has unusually broad balance implications. Another relic slot can add power,
introduce interactions with existing relics, and remove a sacrifice the player
previously had to make between offense, defense, and economy.

Consumable capacity usually provides flexibility and stockpiling rather than
continuous power. Its value increases when the player can reliably acquire,
copy, or replenish consumables.

Capacity increases are not inherently game-breaking, but their value depends
on the entire available catalog. New content can strengthen an existing
capacity upgrade without changing the upgrade itself.

## Overlap Is Part of the Design

A mechanic can support several aspects:

| Mechanic                             | Primary contribution | Possible secondary contributions                 |
| ------------------------------------ | -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| Duplicate a desired card             | Consistency          | Composition, hands.                              |
| Reduce card costs                    | Action capacity      | Tempo, offense, defense.                         |
| Generate vitae from an unplayed card | Economy              | An incentive to retain particular cards in hand. |
| Repeat an attack                     | Offense              | Faster growth from effects that trigger per hit. |
| Raise a matching shield              | Defense              | Future action capacity through banked AP.        |
| Add a relic slot                     | Engine capacity      | Any aspects supported by the additional relic.   |

These connections create the combinatorial space. A build can pursue several
priorities together, and a single mechanic can connect priorities that would
otherwise compete.

## Using the Map to Assess Coverage

Give each mechanic one primary aspect and any meaningful secondary aspects.
Then ask:

- Does each aspect offer meaningful choices and viable build commitments?
- Does it receive direct support, or mostly benefit indirectly?
- Are there several ways to pursue it?
- What must the player sacrifice to prioritize it?
- Which other aspects reinforce it, and which compete with it?
- Does it matter early, late, or throughout the run?

Equal coverage is not the goal. Some aspects naturally need more mechanics
than others, and engine capacity warrants particular restraint. The purpose
is to make deliberate choices about which build priorities the game supports
and how those priorities interact.
